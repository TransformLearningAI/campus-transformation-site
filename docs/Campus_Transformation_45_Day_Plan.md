## The thing we got wrong, and the thing that fixes it

Sarah's second point was the sharp one: *it is unclear who could be in position to take control of a sweeping radical reconstruction of the school's mission, and who would have authority to hire us.*

The answer is nobody. Nobody at a small college has the authority to reconstruct its mission, and the people who collectively do — the full board — meet three or four times a year and are the most frightened people in the building.

But that is only true for the big version. Look at what a college's own delegation-of-authority policy actually reserves for the board:

| Action | Who signs |
|---|---|
| Sale or conveyance of real property | Full board, almost always, at a scheduled meeting |
| Mortgage, encumbrance, new debt | Full board |
| Substantial change to the mission | Full board, plus accreditor approval |
| **Term lease or license of a building** | **Often an officer, alone** |
| **MOU or letter of intent** | **Officer** |
| **Grant application** | **Officer** |
| **Feasibility study contract** | **Officer** |

Tufts caps its VP for Finance at $1M on a *sale* and puts **no stated dollar cap on space leases** signed by the VP for Operations. Cornell's EVP/CFO can execute real estate purchases, sales, leases, mortgages, and easements up to $2M alone. Gettysburg's bylaws bar even the board's own executive committee from acting on property disposal, debt, or the budget — which tells you exactly where the wall is, and where it isn't.

That asymmetry is the whole gradualist strategy in one line: **a lease is the cheap cousin of a sale, and it is frequently the only thing one person can sign.**

The accreditors point the same direction. Sale or lease of real property appears nowhere in the federal substantive-change rule (34 CFR 602.22). SACSCOC does not list property sales or leases as a substantive change and expressly excludes non-educational real estate. MSCHE does not list them as a category. HLC's substantive change policy does not reach them either — its Change of Control policy is about ownership and corporate control of the institution, not property. Only **NECHE** explicitly captures "assumption of debt or significant leases, disposition or acquisition of assets" and requires prior approval six months ahead — so New England schools carry a filing the others do not, and we should know that before we walk in.

The design rule that follows: **a proposal that leases rather than sells, puts no accredited program in the affected space, and does not substantially change the mission is outside substantive change entirely for most of the country, and can often be approved by one person.** Note the word substantial — the rule is triggered by a real change in what the institution does, not by editing the wording of a mission statement. But the practical advice holds: reopening the mission statement invites a fight we do not need.

That is our halfway. Not a smaller version of the big plan. A structurally different ask that a frightened institution can say yes to without anyone having to be brave.

## Workstream B — The Halfway Scan (the method for finding these)

This is the research process Sarah asked for, written so any of us can run it and get the same answer.

Target: **20 verified cases by September 20.** We have 12 already, listed at the end of this document.

### Step 1 — Screen (machine work, one weekend)

IPEDS Finance is the only free complete revenue panel for every college in the country. Private nonprofits file the **F2** file; the revenue lines live in Part D.

| Variable | Line | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| `F2D01` | Tuition and fees, net | Tuition dependence, tracked separately |
| `F2D07` | Local government grants and contracts | High signal — a college taking county or city money is delivering a local service |
| `F2D11` | Sales and services of educational activities | Clinics, farms, testing labs, continuing ed |
| `F2D12` | Sales and services of auxiliary enterprises | Conferences, hospitality, camps, non-student housing |
| `F2D15` | **Other revenue** | **The best single flag.** Genuinely novel revenue has no IPEDS home and lands here |
| `F2D16` | Total revenues | The denominator for every ratio below |

Compute `(F2D07 + F2D11 + F2D12 + F2D15) / F2D16` over ten years for every private nonprofit under 3,000 FTE. Flag any school where that share rose five points or more in a five-year window, total revenue did not fall, and the school is still open. Rank by the size of the `F2D15` and `F2D12` step change.

Caveats that matter: data runs about two years behind, and a jump in `F2D15` can be a one-time asset sale, an insurance settlement, or pandemic-era federal money. The screen produces candidates, never conclusions. Urban Institute's Education Data Portal wraps IPEDS in an API and saves the worst of the file wrangling.

### Step 2 — Confirm the mechanism (Form 990)

The screen says a number moved. The 990 says why.

- **Schedule R, all of Parts I through IV** — this is where a campus real estate LLC, a hotel operating company, or an energy subsidiary shows up by name. Read all four parts, not just one: a **single-member LLC is a disregarded entity and lands in Part I**, a **multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership — the standard structure for a ground-lease development joint venture — lands in Part III**, and Part IV covers related organizations taxable as a corporation or trust. Screening Part IV alone misses most of what we are looking for. Write every entity name down; you will use them in Step 4.
- **Schedule R, Part V** — transactions with related organizations, itemized. Nonzero rent received or asset sales confirm an active real estate relationship.
- **Schedule K, Part III** — private business use of bond-financed property, stated as a percentage. Direct evidence that a campus building was handed to a commercial user, and how much headroom is left.
- **Part VIII** — line 6 gross rents, line 7 net gain on sales of assets other than inventory (real estate sits in the undifferentiated "Other" column alongside equipment, so line 7 tells you *something* was sold, never what — pair it with Schedule D), line 11 other revenue with business codes.
- **Schedule D, Part VI** — land, buildings, equipment year over year. Disposals appear here.

Free at ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer; bulk XML from the IRS if we want to automate. 990s run 10 to 22 months behind, so this confirms, it does not warn.

### Step 3 — Date it and price it (EMMA)

Most private colleges borrow through a state authority, so the bond is titled to the authority and **the college is the "obligor."** Search EMMA by obligor, not issuer. This is why most people conclude their target has no bonds.

Pull the Official Statement's "Plan of Finance" for exactly what was built, and Appendix A for enrollment, discount rate, and a description of the physical plant including leased or jointly developed parcels. Then scan material event notices for one category in particular: **release, substitution, or sale of property securing repayment.** That is a dated flag that campus real estate moved. A free MyEMMA account will send alerts on any obligor we name.

### Step 4 — The local trail (the earliest signal of all)

Campus redevelopment shows up in a planning commission agenda 6 to 24 months before it shows up anywhere else.

- **County assessor:** enumerate every parcel by owner name, plus each LLC from Schedule R. Watch for a campus parcel flipping from **exempt to taxable** — that is a near-certain sign of commercial conversion.
- **County recorder:** grantor-grantee index. Ground leases are recorded as a short **memorandum of lease** — it gives you the parties, the parcel, and the term, never the rent.
- **Planning and zoning agendas:** rezoning, site plan review, special use permits, subdivision of campus parcels, PILOT agreements, TIF creation. Most municipalities run Legistar, Granicus, CivicPlus, or Municode; CivicPlus AgendaCenter has a free per-body RSS feed, which is the cheapest monitoring win available.

### Step 5 — The call (and this is where the actual value is)

Everything above is public because someone had to file it with a regulator or a bond investor. The things that decide whether a model transfers are not in any of it:

- **Rent, escalators, revenue share, reversion, who carried the capital risk.** A recorded lease memorandum never discloses terms. Only the CFO or the developer knows.
- **Why it happened and whether it worked.** The board fight, the donor who objected, the operator who walked.
- **Whether the region's stated need is the real need.** A CEDS priority list is partly a political artifact. Call the workforce board director and the hospital's chief nursing officer.
- **Whether an employer will actually sign.** Labor data says 340 LPN openings. It does not say three employers will fund seats.

Budget **8 to 12 calls per case we intend to publish.** Two conversations with a CFO who did this successfully are worth more than the entire dataset. This is the part AI cannot do, and it is the part that becomes our moat.

## Workstream C — The One-Door Proposal (the method for making our own)

Jeff's line from the thread: *"Then we'd have to think about how to actually make these proposals."* Here is the process. Six steps, and the order is the point.

### Step 1 — Start with the region, not the college

We have been starting with a campus and asking what it could become. Invert it. Ask what the county is short of, then ask whether the campus can supply it. Every source below is free.

| Question | Source | The specific pull |
|---|---|---|
| What does the region say it needs? | The regional **CEDS** — the federally required 5-year strategy from the EDA-designated Economic Development District | The **ranked project priority list**. The region telling you in writing what it wants, with named local sponsors |
| What jobs can't they fill? | **BLS OEWS** by metro and non-metro area | Location quotient **below 1.0** on an occupation with rising statewide employment and above-median wages. That is an unmet local need with a training market |
| Who commutes out, and to what? | **Census LEHD OnTheMap** | Share of employed residents working outside the county, by industry. Heavy out-commuting is demand the region is exporting |
| Is there a housing gap? | **HUD CHAS** | Cost-burdened and severely cost-burdened households by tenure and AMI band, down to census tract |
| Is there a health gap? | **HRSA shortage areas** (refreshed daily) | HPSA designations and **HPSA score** for primary care, dental, mental health |
| Is there a child care gap? | CAP child care deserts, plus the state licensing database | Licensed slots per child under 5. Directly actionable for campus space |
| What's already funded there? | **USAspending.gov** | Filter by place of performance county. Shows who already runs the program you're about to propose |

### Step 2 — Match the need to an asset the campus already has

Dormitory. Commercial kitchen. Gym. Wet lab. Acreage. Fiber. Parking. A commercial kitchen plus a child care desert is a proposal. A half-empty dorm plus a 50-point cost burden number is a proposal. Do not propose building anything.

### Step 3 — Find the money before you find the college

This is the step that makes gradualism financially real. The college should never be asked to write a check.

| Source | Size | Who applies | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **State workforce capital grants** (NY ESD is the model: $35M pool, awards $100K–$3M, 50% state share, renovations and new construction eligible, **nonprofit colleges expressly eligible**) | $100K–$3M | The college | Rounds; NY Round 9 awarded March 2026 |
| **EDA Public Works / Economic Adjustment** | Avg $1.4M / $650K; 60% federal, up to 80% in distressed regions | The college (expressly eligible) or a consortium | **Rolling, no deadline** |
| **USDA Community Facilities** | Grant share **up to 75%**, but only where population is under 5,000 **and** service-area median household income falls below the higher of the poverty line or 60% of the state nonmetro median. Population alone qualifies for nothing — check the income test first, it is what disqualifies most candidate towns | The **municipality** — cleaner path | Continuous |
| **HUD CDBG** | Varies | The municipality; college as subrecipient or CBDO under §570.204 | The city's Action Plan window, not a federal date |
| **LIHTC 4%** | Project-scale | A **developer** partner; the college contributes site control | The bond-financing test dropped from 50% to 25% in 2026 — deals that couldn't pencil now can |
| **New Markets Tax Credits** | $8–15M per building deal | Approach a **CDE**, never Treasury | Made permanent; $10B allocated Dec 2025 |
| **Historic Rehab Tax Credit, 20%** | 20% of qualified rehab | Needs a master lease structure since the college is tax-exempt | Via SHPO, then NPS |
| **PA RACP** (Alvernia's source) | $1M minimum project; 50% non-state match, half secured at submission | The college | Rounds |
| **Section 202 senior housing** | Up to $20M capital advance | **Private nonprofits only — the college's 501(c)(3) is the asset** | Slow; 2–4 years |
| **Community foundation** (the Kresge/Marygrove precedent) | Varies | Us, on the college's behalf | Anytime |

The pattern from Marygrove is worth internalizing: NMTC plus HTC plus foundation money, staged so the college's first commitment was a lease and an MOU, with fee transfer deferred to a later scheduled step.

### Step 4 — Structure it so nobody has to be brave

Before writing a word, obtain two documents, both usually public: the **bylaws article on reserved board powers** and the **signature/delegation-of-authority policy with its threshold appendix.** They tell you who can sign.

Then build the first commitment out of things that sit under officer authority: **a lease or license, an MOU, and a grant application.** Defer any conveyance to a later board-scheduled step. Do not touch the mission statement — that is the single most expensive sentence in any proposal, because it triggers accreditor approval at all four regionals.

Check the accreditor before the meeting: SACSCOC, HLC, and MSCHE do not treat a property lease as a substantive change. NECHE does.

### Step 5 — Write it short, in this order

The Abington adaptive-reuse study is the best published template we have: 61 pages, one building, one dominant recommended use. Ours should run **20 to 30 pages** in the same order:

1. Building and site condition
2. Zoning and regulatory reality
3. Market and need evidence (Step 1's data, cited)
4. Concept — one use, drawn
5. Money — the named program, the match, the applicant, the calendar
6. Governance — who signs what, and when the board is and is not needed
7. Recommendation and the first 90 days

**Lead with community benefit, not with money.** When Albany County's authority put the former Saint Rose campus out to bid, it weighted **community goals alignment at 35%** and **acquisition price at 10%.** That is how public partners actually score. Our proposals should be built to win on the 35%.

### Step 6 — Deliver it to one person, in person

Not the board. Not a committee. One officer who can sign the lease. Sarah's point about secrecy and embarrassment cuts in our favor here: a single-pathway proposal delivered privately to one person is not an admission of anything.

**Target for the 45 days: two finished proposals.** Suggested pairing, one of each type: a Pennsylvania school where RACP is live and we have standing, and a rural school where the USDA Community Facilities route runs through the town rather than the college.

## Sarah's two downsides, revisited

**Scoop risk.** Compression is the hedge, and publishing is the other half of it. A case study published under Jeff's byline in September is a claim staked. Sitting on twenty cases until we feel ready is the actual exposure.

**No money, maybe indefinitely.** Two things change this. The One-Door Proposal is designed so the college never pays — a state workforce capital grant, an EDA award, a municipal CDBG allocation, or a foundation carries the cost. And the fastest first check in this market is a **municipal or authority feasibility study**, not a college engagement. Ashland, Wisconsin hired a consultant for $7,500 to study the Northland campus. The Village of Cazenovia commissioned a full study on a closed college and then won a $10M state Downtown Revitalization award off the back of it. Those buyers are public, their budgets are public, there is no confidentiality friction, and they move in weeks rather than board cycles.

Gradualism does not have to mean unpaid. It means the first yes is small enough for one person to give.
