# Walking the Site as the Client

**Four buyers, one visit each · campustransformation.org · August 2026**

Premise: each reader arrived because their institution needs new revenue and they want a *gradual* answer — not a reinvention, not a funeral. Each walkthrough follows one person from landing to (ideally) engagement, notes where they lean in, where they stall, and what they conclude about the team. As of this writing the live site still serves the old version; each walkthrough notes what the fix pack (`AUDIT_FIXES.md`) changes and what it doesn't. New findings — things the persuasion audit didn't catch — are marked **NEW**.

The clarity-and-ethics section at the end contains verbatim copy, appended to the fix pack as Fixes 11–15.

---

## 1. The VP of Finance

**She arrives** having Googled "college alternative revenue" at 9pm after a budget meeting that ended badly. She reads like an auditor: skips prose, hunts numbers, assumes exaggeration until proven otherwise.

**Today's site:** She hits "You're not failing. The model is." — irrelevant to her; she's not here for absolution, she's here for a revenue line. She scrolls to the eight streams and their dollar ranges, then the five-year table, and stalls hard: no capex line, no methodology, "combined potential $3.5–7.5M" with no worked case. Then the debt claim conflating revenue with debt-service capacity — and now she's not skeptical, she's dismissive. She checks pricing, finds "free for qualifying institutions, typically a $15–20K engagement, always negotiable," and closes the tab having concluded: *enthusiasts, not operators.* **She was the easiest reader to win and today's site loses her fastest.**

**After the fix pack:** Materially better — the capex answer ("it isn't the college's line; here's who actually paid"), net-not-revenue debt language, one pricing sentence, the Purchase College worked example. The funding table on the sample report, with its green/amber "call to confirm" honesty, is the single thing on the whole property most likely to make her forward a link.

**NEW — what's still missing for her:** Nobody on the About page has *done a deal*. Seven advanced degrees, deans, a superintendent — educators, exactly as promised — but she's about to ask "who negotiates the lease?" and the team page has no answer. Don't fabricate one. Say the true thing: the deal work is done with specialized higher-ed counsel and development partners brought in per project (the old FAQ already said this once — "we work with your legal counsel and bring in specialized higher-ed attorneys" — surface it on About, don't bury it in FAQ #11). **Second gap:** she wants something to hand her president. There is no downloadable one-pager anywhere on the site. The sample report's print button is the seed; a "print the two-page summary" version is the deliverable she'd actually use.

**Her engagement path, fixed:** lands → "no capital from the college" holds her → sample report's funding table convinces her → prints the summary → walks it down the hall. Note who converts her: *she* engages the president, not the site. Build for the hand-off.

## 2. The President

**He arrives** on his personal laptop, at home, from a link a trustee sent with no comment. He reads everything, including the blog, because his actual question isn't "what do you sell" — it's "**can these people be trusted with the fact that I looked?**"

**Today's site:** The audit covered his journey; short version: narrated nightmare, death list, relinquish-accreditation FAQ, a blog that writes about prospects. Today's site confirms his fear that engaging is itself a risk. He leaves without a trace.

**After the fix pack:** The register flips — early-mover identity, "what this does not require," a blog policy in writing. Good.

**NEW — the anonymity gap.** Every conversion path on the site requires him to identify himself to a person: "Email Jeff directly," an inquiry form asking his role and institution. For this reader, that's a cliff, not a step. The lookup tool (once live) is the fix — a report with no email required is the first genuinely anonymous engagement — but the site should also carry one **zero-identity intermediate step** today: the sample report's print button, and an "email this page to yourself" that requires nothing but an address. He needs two or three private visits before he's ready to be a name. Design for the return visit: the site's job on visit one is only to be worth a visit two.

**NEW — the peer-proof gap.** The strongest thing anyone could tell him is "a president like you did this and is fine." Cheyney's story is on the page but told as an institution's numbers. Retell one case, one paragraph, president-first: what the president feared, what they signed first, what happened to them personally (Cheyney's president balanced the budget and is celebrated for it). Presidents model on presidents, not on balance sheets.

## 3. The Dean of Admissions / VP of Enrollment

**She arrives** because every revenue conversation at her institution eventually turns into an enrollment target she can't hit, and someone forwarded this "new revenue" site with "thoughts?"

**Today's site — and this is the biggest NEW finding of the walkthrough: the site has nothing for her at all.** The audience selector offers Trustee, President, Municipality, Investor. The inquiry form's role list has no enrollment leader. No sentence anywhere speaks to the person whose life is defined by the enrollment treadmill the site claims to fix. She's mentioned only once — obliquely, in "Your People," where admissions staff are reassigned to "outreach" in a future she reads as her job disappearing. The site accidentally tells the enrollment leader: *this is about a world after you.*

**Why she matters more than the site thinks:** she is often the internal champion with the least to lose and the most to gain. New revenue takes the impossible target off her back. And several pathways are *enrollment engines wearing revenue costumes*: employer-paid training seats become degree-completion pipelines (Dakota Wesleyan's sponsored nursing students ARE enrollment); a health-system partnership feeds clinical placements that recruit; Colby-Sawyer's reset-plus-partnership grew applications 12%. Nobody on the site says the true sentence: **"every pathway here either makes money or makes students, and several make both."** That sentence is hers.

**Fix (added to fix pack as Fix 12):** an enrollment-leader block, her role in the audience selector and the form, and the Dakota Wesleyan / Colby-Sawyer cases retagged with an "enrollment effect" line.

## 4. The Trustee

**He arrives** — retired banker, board member eight years — because he suggested "we should look at other revenue" at the last meeting and got silence, and then went looking himself. He reads as a fiduciary: What's the risk? Who are these people? What would I be signing us up for? What happens if it fails?

**Today's site:** The closure list actually *works* on him — urgency is fuel for a fiduciary in a way it's poison for a president; worth remembering that the death list's move to `/market-context` keeps it findable for exactly him. The governance content wins him. The team page mostly satisfies him. But then he looks for the section every serious prospectus has and this site doesn't: **the risks.**

**NEW — the site never says what can go wrong.** Not once. A tenant can default. A town can fight rezoning. A partner can walk after the renovation. A pathway can underperform. To a fiduciary, a proposal with no risk section isn't clean — it's *unserious*, and it reads like every pitch he's ever thrown out. The most trust-building addition available to this site, for this reader, is an honest "what can go wrong" section — which also happens to be the most ethical addition. It's Fix 13 below, written verbatim.

**NEW — the references problem, handled honestly.** He will eventually ask "who can I call?" The firm is early; there are no completed client engagements to reference. Do not dodge this — pre-empt it, in writing (Fix 14). Early-stage honesty, stated plainly, beats implied experience that diligence unravels. He's a banker; he has seen implied experience unravel.

## 5. What's working — the parts to protect

The **sample report** is the best selling artifact on the property: it demonstrates rather than claims, and the amber "call to confirm" cells do more credibility work than any testimonial could. The **"What this does not require"** section is the single best block of copy for three of the four personas. The **after-the-Scan ladder**, especially "if the Scan finds nothing worth doing, we tell you that instead, free" — keep that sentence forever; it is the site's ethical signature. The **audience selector** mechanic (extend it, per Fix 12). The **Students First** founder quote. And the **candor architecture** — vintages, sources, named programs — which by now is not a feature but the brand.

## 6. Super clear and super ethical — five additions (appended to fix pack as Fixes 11–15)

### Fix 11 — The plain-words box

High on the homepage, before any section that requires scrolling. **VERBATIM:**

> **This site in five sentences.** Your campus probably has one building or one piece of land that could earn money without changing what your college is. We find it, match it to something your region needs, and identify who would pay to build it — usually a government program, an employer, or a development partner, not you. The first step is a free report built from public records. The second is a free two-week study of your specific campus. You can stop at any step, and if we find nothing worth doing, we'll say so.

### Fix 12 — The enrollment leader exists

Add "Enrollment / Admissions Leader" to the audience selector — card copy **VERBATIM:** *"I need the revenue pressure taken off my enrollment number."* — and to the inquiry form's role list. Add one block near the cases, **VERBATIM:**

> **Some pathways make money. Some make students. Several do both.** Employer-sponsored training seats become degree pipelines — at Dakota Wesleyan, a health system pays $20,000 per nursing student who commits to work there after graduation. A health-system partnership brings clinical placements that recruit for you. New revenue isn't a rival to enrollment; done right, it's a feeder — and it means your enrollment target stops being the only thing holding up the budget.

### Fix 13 — "What can go wrong" (the risk section)

On the homepage after the after-the-Scan ladder, and linked from the sample report. **VERBATIM:**

> ## What can go wrong — because something can
>
> A serious proposal names its risks, so here are ours. **A partner can walk** — which is why we structure first commitments as leases and MOUs you can unwind, not conveyances you can't. **A tenant can underperform or default** — which is why the lease, not a revenue projection, defines your downside, and why we never publish projected revenue for your campus. **A town can resist** — which is why community benefit leads every proposal we write, and why we check zoning before you spend anything. **A grant can fall through** — which is why we prefer programs with rolling deadlines and name a second funding path in every proposal. **And the whole idea can simply be wrong for your campus** — which is why the Scan is free, and why "nothing here is worth doing" is an answer we actually give.

### Fix 14 — Early-stage honesty (the references answer)

On the About page, after the team. **VERBATIM:**

> **A note on our track record, because you'll ask.** Campus Transformation is a young practice built by people with long careers inside these institutions. We don't yet have a list of completed client engagements to hand you — what we have is our published research, our case files on colleges that did this successfully, the sample report you can read right now, and a working method we'll demonstrate on your campus for free. We'd rather show you than assure you. And when you're ready to check us out, we'll gladly connect you with people who know our work.

### Fix 15 — The two disclosure lines

15a. Wherever debt, bonds, or accreditation are discussed, add once per page, **VERBATIM:** *"This is general information, not legal or financial advice. Decisions about debt, accreditation, and property belong with your counsel and advisors — we work alongside them, not in place of them."* (Besides being honest, this keeps the debt content safely educational rather than advisory.)

15b. Footer, sitewide, **VERBATIM:** *"We use AI extensively in our research and verify what matters by hand and by phone. We never publish or share who reads, runs a report, or talks to us."* (The AI half is true, differentiating, and better volunteered than discovered; the privacy half is the confidentiality promise made structural.)

## 7. The engagement map, summarized

| Persona | Hook that works | Where they stall today | The artifact that converts them |
|---|---|---|---|
| VP Finance | "No capital from the college" | Unsupported numbers, no deal credentials | Sample report's funding table + printable summary |
| President | "What this does not require" | Every path requires self-identification | The anonymous report, then a president-first case story |
| Enrollment leader | (nothing today — Fix 12) | The site implies her role is obsolete | The "revenue or students, several both" block |
| Trustee | Governance clarity, market urgency | No risk section, no references answer | "What can go wrong" + the early-stage honesty note |

The pattern across all four: **this site converts nobody directly.** It converts the reader into an internal advocate who converts someone else — the CFO prints for the president, the trustee forwards to the chair, the enrollment leader champions to the cabinet. Every fix above serves that: make each persona's hand-off artifact excellent, and let the people do the persuading.
