# Persuasion Audit: campustransformation.org

**Logic, marketing, psychology, and emotional appeal — reviewed against every audience the site courts · August 2026**

Method: full-site content capture (homepage both variants, About, Process, Cases, Blog, Inquiry), read through the eyes of five readers: a president, a board chair, a CFO, a municipal/EDC official, and a foundation or investor. Quotes are verbatim from the live site.

The one-sentence verdict: **the site's biggest persuasion problem is that it is two websites arguing with each other** — the old comprehensive-makeover site and the new gradualist site are both live, and the old one contains sentences that destroy the new one's promises.

---

## A. Fix-first: five things actively costing you prospects

### A1. The accreditation FAQ contradicts your central promise, in writing

Old-site FAQ #2, verbatim: *"What happens to accreditation?" — "You **voluntarily relinquish it** as part of the transition."*

The new page promises, in a headline: *"No accreditation change."*

A president who reads both — and a nervous one will read everything — concludes you are hiding the real plan behind the soft one. This is not a tone problem; it is a written contradiction on the most frightening subject in the reader's world. Relinquishing accreditation means, to a president, "my college stops being a college." Under the gradualist strategy this FAQ must say what the strategy actually holds: most pathways require no accreditation action at all, and the comprehensive model is one option at the far end of a spectrum, not the destination.

Same problem in "What you might leave behind: some or all accreditation compliance, federal financial aid paperwork, the enrollment treadmill." Calling the loss of Title IV eligibility "paperwork" insults a reader who knows exactly what federal aid is. Euphemism reads as either ignorance or manipulation, and this audience assumes manipulation.

### A2. Your proof section proves the opposite of your headline

The cases page headline: *"Real examples of campuses that **transformed instead of closing**."*

The three real examples: Marygrove — **closed 2019**. Goddard — **closed**, campus sold to a developer. Saint Rose — **closed 2024**, campus taken over by a county land authority.

Every flagship proof point is a college that died and whose building found a second life. To a board fighting for institutional survival, these cases whisper: *the college doesn't survive this; the real estate does.* That is precisely the fear Sarah's memo identified, and the site's own evidence confirms it. The Cheyney/Purchase/Warren Wilson cases on the new page fix this exactly — colleges that stayed colleges — but the old cases page is still live and still the /cases URL. The closed-campus stories aren't worthless; they belong under a heading like "what happens to campuses whose boards waited," which is honest about what they actually show.

### A3. The "Happening Now" death list and the named modeled plans

Two related problems. The closure list ("Fontbonne — Closed 2025 · Siena Heights — Closing 2026...") is a memento-mori wall that terrifies the reader you want to reassure, and it publicly labels named institutions as dying — institutions whose leaders you may later court, and who will remember the list.

Worse: the "modeled transformation plans" publish invented after-plans with revenue projections for **real, named schools** — Pittsburgh Technical College, and Hampshire College with "$2.55M Year 1" attached. Every prospect who sees this learns the lesson: *engage with these people and your campus becomes their public case study.* It directly undercuts the inquiry page's promise that "all information is confidential." Convert the modeled plans to anonymized composites ("a 400-student faith-affiliated college in the rural Midwest" — the third one already does this correctly), or get written consent to use names.

### A4. The blog breaks your confidentiality promise in public

Blog post, June 22: *"Your First Client Will Almost Kill You — We built Campus Transformation to save struggling colleges. **Our first real prospect is a school that can't make payroll.**"*

On the same domain where the inquiry form says "all information is confidential." Any president doing diligence reads the blog — diligence is what presidents do — and finds that prospects get written about. "Oakland City Doesn't Have to Die" names an active outreach target in a headline with death in it. The blog is good thinking-out-loud, but it needs one editorial rule, permanently: **never blog about a prospect, a client, or an identifiable institution you might ever want in the room.** The confidentiality promise extends to content marketing or it doesn't exist.

### A5. Two homepages, two free offers, one broken conversion path

The main URL serves the old page; `?v=2` serves the new. Search engines, shared links, and returning visitors will land on either — and they describe different companies with different theories of the case. Pick one (the new one), 301-redirect the other, and archive the old copy.

Related tangles: the site now has **two named free offers** — "Community X-Ray: Free for qualifying institutions. Typically a $15-20K engagement" (which manages to be free and $20K in one breath) and the "One-Pathway Scan: free." One free offer, one name. Keep Community X-Ray as the internal method name it was always meant to be. And the Process page's "Start the Conversation" button links to `/campus-transformation#contact`, **which 404s** — a dead end at the exact moment of conversion.

## B. Logic flaws a CFO or bond trustee will catch

**B1. Revenue is not debt-service capacity.** "A campus generating $5-7M in revenue can service $1-2M in annual bond payments" conflates top line with net. The site's own five-year table shows $7.5M revenue producing $3.3M net — so the claim survives arithmetic, but only via a table the reader must cross-reference, and the FAQ version omits the costs entirely. Any bond trustee reads "revenue" where "net operating income" belongs and marks the author as unfamiliar with credit. One word fixes it.

**B2. The five-year table has no capital expenditure line.** Renovating buildings for eight new uses costs money before it makes money; the table shows operating cost only. A CFO's first question — "who pays for the build-out?" — is unanswered on the page, even though the *actual* answer (grants, partners, developers — not the college) is the strongest card in the new strategy's hand. The old page never plays it.

**B3. Timeline contradiction between pages.** FAQ #8: "Self-sustaining: **2-3 years**." Process page stat block: "**12-18 Months** to self-sustaining." Both live. Small, but this is exactly the class of inconsistency a skeptical trustee collects as evidence of loose thinking.

**B4. False precision, everywhere.** Eight streams with tight dollar ranges; component projections to the $50K ("Faith & Community Center — $50K, grant-funded"); "WIOA funding (up to $15,000 per student)" stated as a national fact when ITA caps are set board-by-board and a workforce director knows it. Numbers this precise without visible methodology read as template math. The go-to-market review already made the fix: show the arithmetic for one worked, sourced example (Purchase College's $2M/year is public and real) and let honest ranges do the rest.

**B5. A stale fear stat carrying the biggest claim.** "65% of small privates showing financial stress" is attributed to Forbes Financial Health Grades — a dataset that has not been refreshed in years. The scariest number on the page should be the best-sourced number on the page. The NPR/442-institutions projection you already use elsewhere is stronger and current.

## C. Psychology, audience by audience

**The president.** The old page opens by narrating their nightmare in second person: *"Your enrollment is declining. Your endowment can't cover the gap. Your board is looking at closure — selling the campus, laying off your people."* The writer's intent is empathy; the effect on a person in what Sarah's memo called "secrecy, embarrassment, desperation" is exposure — being seen. The reflex is denial and a closed tab. Even the absolution line, "You're not failing. The model is," addresses the reader as someone whose failure needs excusing. The new page's register — "You do not have to be in crisis to read this page" — is the correct psychology: it lets the reader adopt the identity of an early mover rather than confess the identity of a failure. The gradualist rewrite should finish the job the new page started, everywhere.

**The board chair.** Here the new page carries its own risk, and it's worth naming plainly: four headers that begin "No board vote..." can read, to a governance-minded chair, as *"we teach your president to route around you."* Ken Bado reads that section too. The content is right; the framing must be **sequencing, not circumvention**: "within the authority your board has already delegated" / "so that what reaches your board is a working proposal, not a blank question." One added sentence — *"Boards get better decisions when the first version arrives already de-risked"* — converts the chair from threat-detector to beneficiary.

**The CFO.** Covered in section B. One addition: "our fees are on a sliding scale and always negotiable" reads as haggling, and haggling reads as desperation. "Scoped and priced before you commit," already on the new page, is the right sentence — make it the only pricing sentence on the site.

**The municipality/EDC.** "Don't let the campus leave your community" is the best line in the old CTA block — loss-framed, correctly aimed, urgent without being insulting, because the *town* isn't the one failing. This audience can be pushed harder than the college audience, and the site under-serves them: they get one CTA card and no page. Given that municipal feasibility studies are the fastest first check in this market, the town deserves its own landing section: what a reuse study costs, what Ashland did, what the town gets. Fear appeals that are toxic for presidents are *motivating* for mayors.

**The foundation/investor.** "Campus transformation is an impact investment with real returns" sits one scroll away from "always negotiable" and a P&L with no capex. This reader needs exactly three things the site never assembles in one place: the Marygrove precedent (philanthropy-led, $50M+, real), the funding-stack logic (NMTC + HTC + program grants), and evidence of deal flow. The lookup tool, once live, *is* the deal-flow evidence.

**Everyone at once — the buried trust asset.** The About page is quietly the strongest page on the site: seven advanced degrees, a superintendent who ran a $137M district and 108 schools, a Duke associate dean, 25 years at La Roche. None of it surfaces on either homepage, where trust is actually decided. Meanwhile the homepage spends its credibility budget on "We're not McKinsey" — which names the premium competitor, invites the comparison, and sounds defensive — and "Your campus is the venue. Our technology is the engine," which tells a proud institution it's about to become someone else's platform. Cut both. Replace with one line and five names: *"Built by people who ran colleges, school systems, and workforce programs — not a real estate firm."*

**The inquiry form's confession problem.** To request the free Scan, the form asks the reader to classify themselves: "Under financial distress / Considering closure / Already closed." That's a confession gate on a free offer — the psychological price is exactly what the gradualist strategy exists to remove. Make "current situation" optional, reorder so the least-frightening options lead ("Planning ahead / Exploring alternatives"), and let the Scan intake stay what the copy promises: "which building is least used — that is the entire intake."

## D. What's working — protect these

The "Students First" section is the moral center of the old site and its founder quote ("A transformation that leaves students stranded isn't a transformation — it's an abandonment") is the single most trustworthy paragraph anywhere on the domain. The new page is all pragmatics and could use one sentence of this warmth — care is a differentiator no data module supplies. "Just two people who care about education talking about what's possible" is the right closing register for the FAQ. The six principles on About ("The campus belongs to the community" / "We stay") are good and underexposed. The audience-segmented CTAs are the right mechanic. And the new page's honesty architecture — vintages on every number, "call to confirm" where the data is incomplete, "if the Scan finds nothing, we tell you, free" — is the site's emerging competitive identity: **the only vendor in this market that shows its uncertainty.** Keep building on that; it is worth more than any projection.

6. **One pricing sentence, one free-offer name, sitewide** (A5, C-CFO).
7. **Surface the team on the homepage; cut "not McKinsey" and "venue/engine"** (C).
8. **Soften the inquiry form's self-classification** (C).
9. **Fix the CFO-facing numbers**: net-not-revenue for debt service, a capex/who-pays line, reconcile the two self-sustaining timelines, re-source or replace the 65% stat (B).
