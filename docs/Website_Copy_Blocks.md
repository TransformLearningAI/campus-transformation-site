## The problem with the site as it stands

The site is built for a school that already knows it is dying. "Don't close. Transform." "You're not failing. The model is." A "Happening Now" list of seven campuses closing. It is honest, it is well written, and under the new strategy it is aimed at exactly the wrong reader.

Sarah's argument is that the schools worth studying and serving are the ones **still functioning** while finding new revenue. Those schools cannot be seen reading a site about closure. A president who clicks "Talk to Us — Free & Confidential" from a work laptop has, in his own mind, admitted something. The current site asks a frightened person to take the largest possible first step.

The fix is not to soften the message. It is to change who the front door is for, and to make the first step small enough that taking it is not an admission.

## BLOCK 1 — Hero (replaces "Don't close. Transform." and its subhead)

**Headline:**

> ## Start with one building.

**Subhead:**

> Most colleges under financial pressure are handed the same advice: reinvent everything, or close. There is a third option almost nobody talks about. Find one underused building or one unused parcel, match it to something the region is actually short of, and let somebody else pay to build it. One pathway. One signature. No grand plan.

**Primary CTA:** `See what one building could do →`

**Secondary CTA:** `Look up your school`

**Keep "Don't close. Transform." as the tagline near the logo.** It is a good line and it is ours. It just should not be the first thing a nervous president reads.

## BLOCK 2 — New section, place it second (this is the most important addition)

> ## What this does not require

> **No board vote, in most cases.** A term lease or license of one building typically sits inside the authority already delegated to a president or CFO. Selling property almost always requires the full board. Leasing it often does not. We will read your delegation-of-authority policy and tell you which side of that line your project falls on before you take it to anyone.

> **No accreditation change.** Sale or lease of real property is not a substantive change under federal rules, and is not a reportable category for SACSCOC, HLC, or MSCHE. New England schools accredited by NECHE do carry a filing on significant leases and asset dispositions, and we will tell you that up front.

> **No change to your mission.** That is the one thing we will not ask you to touch. A substantial change to an institution's mission requires accreditor approval, and reopening the mission statement starts a board fight on top of it. Every pathway here is designed to work with your mission exactly as written.

> **No capital from the college.** In every model on this page, someone else funded the building: a developer, a state workforce grant, a federal rural facilities program, a health system, a community foundation, an employer. Your contribution is the site.

*Why this block matters: nothing on the current site tells a president that the first step is small and reversible. This is the single most persuasive thing we can say, and right now we say none of it.*

## BLOCK 3 — New section, replaces "Happening Now"

> ## Colleges doing this right now — and still open

> These are not turnaround stories or closure stories. They are colleges that stayed colleges and found money somewhere other than tuition.

> **Cheyney University, Pennsylvania.** Had close to 400,000 square feet of building space sitting unoccupied or underused, and opened it to private tenants. Eight companies are on campus now — agribusiness, cancer research, solar manufacturing, additive manufacturing — alongside stadium and auditorium rentals. A $7.4 million deficit became a $2.1 million surplus, the first balanced budget in eight years.

*Note for whoever pastes this: do not say "leased 400,000 square feet." Cheyney's own page says close to 400,000 square feet stood unoccupied or underutilized. That is the inventory they marketed, not the amount under lease, and they publish no leased square footage or lease revenue. Also do not attach Cheyney's 2025 enrollment and retention numbers to this paragraph — they are real and impressive, but they are six years after the surplus and no source connects them to the leasing program.*

> **Purchase College, New York.** Ground-leased 40 acres to a senior living community, financed with public bonds. No college capital at risk. The college collects $2 million a year, which state law directs to student scholarships and new faculty lines.

> **Warren Wilson College, North Carolina.** Sold 191 of its 1,100 acres to a land conservancy — with an agreement that keeps the college grazing the pasture and teaching in the forest. $4.7 million, against a $5.5 million deficit. They sold the asset without losing the use of it.

> **Goucher College, Maryland.** Ground-leased three acres out of 287 to a neighboring senior living operator for 127 apartments. The college put in no construction capital.

> **D'Youville University, New York.** Built a health professions building where a regional health system staffs and operates a community clinic inside it. The health system raised $5.07 million toward the project.

> **Lackawanna College, Pennsylvania.** A $10 million technology center funded by state money, the Appalachian Regional Commission, and a private energy company. Separately, employers pay tuition directly for their own employees.

> **Dakota Wesleyan University, South Dakota.** A regional health system pays $20,000 per nursing student for the junior and senior years in exchange for a three-year work commitment. The health system asked for it.

> `Read the full case files →`

*Move the closing-campus list to a subpage titled "The market context" and link it from the FAQ. It belongs on the site. It does not belong on the homepage under the new strategy — it brands us as a closure firm and it frightens exactly the reader we now want.*

## BLOCK 4 — Replaces "The Situation You're In" / "You're not failing. The model is."

> ## You do not have to be in crisis to read this page

> Most of what is written about small colleges is written about the ones in trouble. This is for the ones that are still working — enrolling students, making payroll, doing it on a tighter margin every year — and would rather add a revenue line now than have the conversation later.

> Everything here works better early. A college with three good years left has options a college with three good months does not: it can negotiate a lease instead of accepting an offer, choose a partner instead of taking the only one, and move on its own calendar.

## BLOCK 5 — Reframes "The New Model" (the eight revenue streams)

Keep the eight streams. Change the framing above them and add a sort.

> ## Eight ways a campus earns without becoming something else

> You do not need eight. You need one. Below, each is tagged by how much it asks of you — because the right first move is almost always the one that requires the fewest signatures.

Tag each stream with one of:

- **`Start here`** — a lease or license, officer-level signature, no capital from the college
- **`Partner-funded`** — requires an outside developer, employer, or health system, and their money
- **`Board decision`** — involves conveying property or taking on debt

Order them so every `Start here` stream appears first.

## BLOCK 7 — Fix on the "Financials" section

The five-year projections and the $3.5–7.5 million combined range are the most skippable thing on the site, because one skeptical CFO dismisses the whole page on them. Do not remove them. Do three things:

1. Add a methodology note directly under the table: what the ranges assume, and that they are illustrative rather than a forecast.
2. Add **one worked example with a real citation** — Purchase College's $2 million a year on a 40-acre ground lease is public, verifiable, and does more work than every projected number combined.
3. Add a line of self-awareness: *"These are ranges, not promises. The only numbers on this site we will defend absolutely are the ones with a source next to them."*
